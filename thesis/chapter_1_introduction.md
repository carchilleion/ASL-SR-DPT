CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 1.1 BACKGROUND OF THE STUDY In digital image processing, handheld and edge consumer devices face severe physical and hardware constraints that compromise sensor quality. Under low-light conditions, raw smartphone sensors capture fewer photons, introducing high-intensity thermal and electronic noise that degrades spatial clarity (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018). This noise is conventionally modeled as Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN) in the spatial domain (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018). While standard spatial denoising filters—such as bilateral, median, and anisotropic diffusion filters—are highly effective at suppressing high-frequency noise, they inevitably smooth out critical high-frequency structural edges and fine textures (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018). The loss of these structural boundaries degrades image sharpness and spatial resolution, presenting a major barrier to high-fidelity consumer photography and mobile-based computational imaging. To preserve sharp edge transitions while filtering out noise, Compressive Sensing (CS) theory provides a powerful mathematical alternative. CS asserts that natural images exhibit approximate sparsity when projected onto appropriate transform domains, such as the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) or Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) (Candès & Wakin, 2008). This sparse representation allows the denoising task to be modeled as an optimization problem over an underdetermined linear system (Baraniuk, 2007). Standard convex relaxation methods, such as Basis Pursuit Denoising (BPDN) and LASSO, substitute the non-convex L ₀ norm with a convex L ₁ proxy (Tibshirani, 1996; Chen et al., 2001). While the L ₁ -norm optimization ensures a convex landscape and a guaranteed global minimum, it requires computationally expensive algorithms—such as the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)—to solve a large system of linear equations at each iteration (Boyd et al., 2011). This computational burden can increase processing latency and resource utilization on resource-constrained devices, motivating the investigation of computationally efficient sparse-recovery methods. Recent advancements in military radar signal processing have successfully utilized high-speed, non-convex single-loop solvers to recover high-resolution Inverse Synthetic Aperture Radar (ISAR) images (Feng et al., 2021). Algorithms such as the Improved Single-Loop Smoothed L ₀ (ISSL ₀ ) and Fast Approximate L ₀ (FAL ₀ ) optimize the reconstruction path by approximating the discontinuous, NP-hard L ₀ norm using smooth, continuous Gaussian functions (Mohimani et al., 2009; Jin et al., 2019). This approximation allows the optimization problem to be solved using gradient descent or modified Newton methods within a single, unified loop, bypassing the expensive nested "inner-outer" loops characteristic of traditional continuation frameworks (Mohimani et al., 2009; Feng et al., 2021). However, transferring these single-loop radar algorithms to natural 2D image denoising under compressive sensing constraints introduces severe mathematical and physical limitations: The DC-Scrambling Problem: In military ISAR imaging, the target is strictly sparse (a few reflective metal points against a silent, empty sky background) (Feng et al., 2021). Natural 2D images are generally compressible or approximately sparse in transform domains such as DCT or wavelets, with a small number of dominant coefficients and many lower-amplitude coefficients

Random sensing may increase reconstruction error in the DC component because the local mean is represented indirectly through compressed measurements. Optimization Instability: The non-convex landscape of the smoothed L ₀ approximation is highly susceptible to local minima in underdetermined, noisy settings. Without a mathematically rigorous line-search and adaptive regularization framework, gradient descent steps can easily overshoot the optimal coefficients, causing divergence or false convergence stalls. This study directly addresses these challenges by developing a mathematically consistent, single-loop hybrid sparse recovery solver. The proposed solver incorporates an unconstrained regularized objective function, a stable Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)-based pseudoinverse initialization, and a mathematically rigorous Armijo backtracking line search with corrected failure-path continuation logic. Furthermore, to reduce boundary discontinuities and block-related artifacts, the solver is integrated into an overlapping patch-reconstruction pipeline utilizing a dense stride and a normalized 2D Hamming Window weighting scheme. A distinct DC-preserving compressive sensing experiment is also established, offering a practical speed-efficiency trade-off tailored for low-power mobile edge processors. 1.2 REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE (THEMATIC SYNTHESIS) To anchor the proposed hybrid solver, the related literature is synthesized across four distinct mathematical and technological domains: 1.2.1 Convex Relaxation and Greedy Baselines in Compressive Sensing The mathematical core of sparse signal recovery is governed by the underdetermined linear system: y = Az + e where y ∈ ℝ ᴹ represents the compressed measurement vector, A ∈ ℝ ᴹˣᴺ is the underdetermined sensing matrix (with M < N), z ∈ ℝ ᴺ represents the sparse transform-domain coefficients, and e is measurement noise (Candès & Tao, 2005; Donoho & Elad, 2003). To solve this underdetermined system, traditional convex relaxation methods replace the discontinuous L ₀ norm with the convex L ₁ norm (Tibshirani, 1996). Under the LASSO framework, the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) decomposes the global optimization problem into smaller, decoupled subproblems (Boyd et al., 2011). Although ADMM guarantees stable convergence, it requires the repeated solution of a linear system involving (AᵀA + ρ I). When the system matrix is fixed, its factorization can be precomputed and reused across iterations (Boyd et al., 2011). The repeated solution of the resulting linear system contributes substantial computational cost, particularly when the system is large or when many overlapping image patches must be processed. Conversely, greedy algorithms like Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP) avoid joint optimization by sequentially identifying the dictionary columns (atoms) that exhibit the highest correlation with the current residual (Tropp & Gilbert, 2007). In each step, OMP projects the residual onto the selected support set and solves a least-squares problem (Needell & Vershynin, 2009). While

OMP is highly effective at isolating dominant, high-contrast edges in synthetic blocks, its sequential projections are computationally heavy when scaling to thousands of overlapping image patches on low-power mobile CPUs (Davis, 1997; Chen et al., 2001). 1.2.2 Non-Convex Smoothed L ₀ Continuation and Single-Loop Architectures To bypass both the expensive matrix inversions of ADMM and the sequential dictionary searches of OMP, Mohimani et al. (2009) introduced the Smoothed L ₀ (SL ₀ ) algorithm. SL ₀ approximates the discrete L ₀ norm using a continuous, parameterized Gaussian function: |z| ₀ ≈ N - Σ e^(-zᵢ² / 2σ²) where the continuation parameter σ dictates the smoothness of the approximation (Mohimani et al., 2009). Historically, SL ₀ was implemented as a double-loop algorithm: the outer loop slowly decays σ to trace a path toward the sharp, non-convex L ₀ norm, while the inner loop executes multiple gradient descent steps to reach convergence for that fixed σ (Mohimani et al., 2009). To eliminate the redundant computational cycles of the inner loop, single-loop architectures—such as the Improved Single-Loop Smoothed L ₀ (ISSL ₀ ) and Combined Regularized Smooth L ₀ (CReSL ₀ )—were developed for high-resolution radar imaging (Feng et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019). By updating the continuation parameter σ geometrically at every single iteration, these solvers achieve substantial speedups (Wang et al., 2019; Quan et al., 2024). However, applying these radar solvers to natural images introduces severe optimization instabilities, as natural signals contain small, non-zero AC coefficients that are aggressively driven to zero by the L ₀ penalty, causing representation loss and spatial blurring (Wimalajeewa et al., 2012; Babacan et al., 2010). 1.2.3 Active-Set Methods and Support Pruning (FAL ₀ ) To further reduce the mathematical overhead of iterative solvers, active-set methods restrict gradient evaluations to a subset of estimated non-zero coefficients. The Fast Approximate L ₀ (FAL ₀ ) algorithm utilizes a positive definite matrix to stabilize convergence and incorporates a "Zero-Element Neglect" strategy (Jin et al., 2019). By identifying inactive coefficients (whose magnitudes fall below a threshold proportional to σ) and excluding them from subsequent gradient updates, the solver dramatically reduces floating-point operations (FLOPs) (Jin et al., 2019). Despite these computational savings, hard active-set methods suffer from permanent coefficient deletion: once a coefficient is falsely flagged as zero and pruned, its gradient is ignored, and it can never be recovered (Jin et al., 2019). This leads to severe visual degradation in natural images, where soft textures and low-amplitude edges are permanently wiped out. The ASL-SR-DPT implementation mitigates this limitation by periodically reopening the active support, allowing previously excluded coefficients to re-enter subsequent optimization iterations. 1.2.4 Spatial Image Reconstruction and Overlapping Patch Aggregation In digital image denoising, processing images block-by-block is standard practice to preserve local spatial statistics (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018). However, independent reconstruction of

To mitigate this, state-of-the-art pipelines decompose images using an overlapping stride and aggregate the overlapping patches using weighted windowing (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018). Multiplying overlapping spatial patches by a 2D Hamming Window and dividing by the accumulated spatial weight canvas ensures smooth, continuous boundaries (Gonzalez & Woods, 2018; Li & Qi, 2015). This digital signal processing standard is highly compatible with sparse-recovery solvers, helping to reduce boundary discontinuities and block-related artifacts and recover visual quality. 1.3 THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK The theoretical foundation of this study is anchored in non-convex continuous optimization and compressive sampling theory, synthesizing the concepts of graduated continuation, sufficient descent, and active-set support restriction. The theoretical framework rests on three foundational pillars. First, Compressive Sampling Theory (Candès & Wakin, 2008) models underdetermined recovery as y = Az + e, establishing the information-theoretic limits of random projections. Second, Graduated Non-Convexity (Mohimani et al., 2009) approximates the discrete L ₀ norm using continuous Gaussian functions and employs a continuation parameter (σ) to smooth local minima. Finally, Armijo-type sufficient descent provides a principled acceptance criterion for candidate steps for the current objective and active support. It does not by itself establish global convergence of the complete nonconvex continuation algorithm. Building on these principles, the proposed hybrid model defines the objective function as min_z 0.5 * ||Az - y|| ₂ ² - λ * Σ( e^(-zᵢ² / 2σ²)). This model incorporates several key components: SVD-based Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse initialization (Pinit = A†), single-loop geometric continuation with σ updating per iteration, active-set zero-element neglect with periodic support re-evaluation, and Armijo backtracking line search with corrected failure-path logic. 1.3.1 The Unconstrained Regularized Objective Function Rather than forcing exact linear projections (Az = y), which preserves noise in the reconstructed image, this study utilizes an unconstrained regularized model: L(z) = ½||Az - y|| ₂ ² - λ Σ e^(-zᵢ² / 2σ²) This formulation balances the L ₂ data fidelity term (which minimizes residual reconstruction error) with a negative Gaussian smoothed L ₀ penalty (which drives coefficients to exact zeros to promote sparsity). The regularization parameter λ is treated as a controlled hyperparameter whose value is selected through a documented validation procedure. 1.3.2 The Unified Gradient The search direction of the solver is guided by the analytically derived unified gradient of the unconstrained objective function: ∇ L(z) = Aᵀ(Az - y) + λ (z / σ²) ⊙ e^(-z² / 2σ²) where ⊙ denotes the Hadamard element-wise product. For the current active support, the analytical gradient provides the steepest descent direction with respect to the active variables. 1.3.3 SVD-Based Minimum-Norm Initialization

At initialization (k = 0), the initial coefficient vector is set as the minimum-norm least-squares estimate: z ⁽⁰⁾ = Pinity. For each fixed sensing-matrix configuration, the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse (Pinit = A† = V Σ† Uᵀ) is computed once and reused for patch initialization. This precomputation ensures a highly stable starting point close to the true coefficients, avoiding repeated pseudoinverse construction for every image or experiment. 1.3.4 Armijo Backtracking and Corrected Failure-Path Logic To support numerical stability and descent, the step size μ is governed by the Armijo condition (Nocedal & Wright, 2006): L(z + μ d) ≤ L(z) + c ⋅ μ ⋅ ∇ L(z)ᵀd where the search direction d = - ∇ L(z), c = 10 ⁻⁴ is the Armijo descent constant, and the step decay factor β = 0.5. When backtracking fails, the solver executes a corrected failure-path routine to reduce the risk of unstable step updates and preserve the current optimization state: It retains the coefficient vector: znew = z. It decays the persistent step size: μ ← μ × 0.5. It skips the continuation decay of σ, preserving the current objective landscape. It bypasses the convergence relative-change calculation, eliminating false-convergence loops. 1.3.5 Zero-Element Neglect with Periodic Support Re-evaluation To slash floating-point complexity, the solver restricts gradient evaluations to the active set: active_indices = |zᵢ| > 10 ⁻⁵ σ Subsequent matrix-vector operations are performed strictly over the active sub-matrix Aactive and sub-vector zactive. To prevent the permanent deletion of critical image details, the solver implements a Periodic Support Re-evaluation schedule: every 3 iterations, the active mask is reset to all True values, allowing temporarily suppressed coefficients to re-enter the optimization space if their gradient changes. 1.4 CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK AND RESEARCH PARADIGM The conceptual framework translates the theoretical optimization steps into an applied software architecture. This is achieved by designing, validating, and prototyping a single-loop hybrid sparse recovery solver that evaluates the trade-off between reconstruction quality and computational cost. Specifically, the study addresses the following research objectives: Formulate the unconstrained regularized objective function and analytically derive its unified gradient to model noisy compressive measurements without forcing exact projections. Design an Armijo-type backtracking line search and a graduated continuation schedule to dynamically tune the descent step size ( ) and prevent optimization divergence. Develop an active-set support restriction scheme (Zero-Element Neglect) with periodic support re-evaluation to reduce floating-point operations (FLOPs) without permanently pruning vital image coefficients. Integrate a stable precomputation framework utilizing the SVD-based Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse strictly for initial minimum-norm estimation. Establish an overlapping patch-reconstruction pipeline utilizing a dense extraction stride and a normalized 2D Hamming Window weighting scheme to reduce boundary discontinuities and block-related artifacts. Develop a functional prototype web application based on an Agile Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model to transition the simulated mathematical solver into a practical, interactive computational tool. 1.6 SIGNIFICANCE OF THE STUDY This study provides direct, practical, and theoretical benefits to several key stakeholders: Mobile Software and Camera Application Developers: Provides a lightweight, mathematically stable, and computationally evaluated sparse recovery engine that can be compiled for low-power mobile operating systems whose computational characteristics may inform future deployment on resource-constrained mobile or edge devices.
